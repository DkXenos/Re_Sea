## 0. The one thing judges must remember

**Pitch line:**
> *A claw machine pulls trash off the seabed. At the sorting desk, finding out
> what something really is costs you, and only what goes in the right bin
> becomes material to rebuild the bay.*

**Three pillars.** Every slide should support at least one of these.

1. **The deciding detail is often invisible, and finding it costs something.**
   Tools cost shift time, and a tool that finds nothing still costs 4.
2. **The environmental message is the rule of the game, not just the setting.**
   A wrong bin gives **zero** material, not less material.
3. **Mistakes teach instead of punish.** There are no lives, fines or game over.
   At the end of the day the game shows exactly which property you **never
   checked**.

**Say this line word for word** (it's from proposal §2.2.7):
> *"A quiz tells you the right answer. Re:Sea tells you what you should have
> checked."*

**Closing line** (this is the game's own last narration line):2``2
> *"Lower the claw. Look before you decide."*

## 2. Real cases to use

that one contaminated item gets a whole batch rejected, has no source at all.
These cases fill that gap. Every one has been checked against its source (see
[Sources](#sources)).

| # | Case | What it proves | Use on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Surabaya, 2019.** At Surabaya, 8 containers declared as *waste paper* (more than 210 tonnes, from Australia) turned out to hold household rubbish, used nappies and hazardous material. Indonesia sent them back. That year, dozens of contaminated containers went back to the US, France, Germany, Australia and Hong Kong. | Mixed-in contamination gets the **whole shipment** rejected, not discounted. This is the real version of our zero-value rule, and it happened in **our own city**. | Slide 2 (the opening story), slide 7 |

| 3 | **Jambeck et al., 2015 (*Science*).** Indonesia was estimated as the 2nd-largest source of marine plastic after China, at 0.48–1.29 million tonnes a year. | The scale of the problem, from an internationally known source. | Slide 2 |
| 4 | **Indonesian government data.** Plastic leaking into the sea fell from 615,675 t (2018) to 359,061 t (2023), a 41.68% drop. The national target was a **70%** cut by 2025. | The problem is current and measurable, and the country is still short of its own target. | Slide 2 |
| 5 | **BPS 2018 Environmental Indifference Index (IPKLH).** Waste handling scored 0.72, the worst of the four areas measured. Its questions include sorting organic waste and handling hazardous household waste. | Sorting is where Indonesian households are least engaged. | Slide 2 |

**How to tell the story (slide 2):**
> *"In 2019, eight containers labelled 'waste paper' arrived in Surabaya, our
> city. Inside were nappies and hazardous waste. The whole shipment was sent
> back. It wasn't worth less; it was rejected. That's the rule at the heart of
> Re:Sea: a wrong bin isn't a smaller payout, it's nothing."*

**Be careful with:**
- **Jambeck** estimated 2010 data and was published in 2015. Show the year, and
  pair it with the government's 2023 figure so the problem looks current.
  Jambeck and the government measured in different ways, so **don't subtract one
  from the other or present them as a trend**.
- **BPS 0.72 is an index from 0 to 1, not "72% of people".** Some media reported
  it as a percentage, so don't repeat that.
- **Don't say "most people already care" on stage.** Proposal §1.1.2 says it, but
  there's no source, and the BPS index points the other way. A framing that
  holds up: *"When sorting feels like guesswork, people stop trying. Re:Sea
  turns the guesswork into a skill."*

**The problem-to-mechanic table from the proposal (§1.1.3).** It's the
strongest single visual for slide 2 or 7:

| Real-world problem | How the game answers it |
|---|---|
| The deciding detail can't be seen from outside | Hidden properties; revealing them needs a tool that costs shift time |
| People sort without checking because checking is a hassle | Guessing is allowed, but a wrong guess yields zero |
| People don't know where their reasoning went wrong | The end-of-day report shows which property was never checked |
| Some waste can still be saved | Rinse recovers stained items, but not all of them, and not always |

### Slide 4: Core mechanics
Use one panel per mechanic:

1. **Claw machine:** move along the rail and drop. Grip chance depends on
   weight (70% for a 2 kg glass bottle, 95% for dry leaves). The cost is paid
   when the claw starts down, so a failed grip still costs it.
2. **Shift time:** one budget per day, spent **only by actions**. There's no
   countdown, so thinking, reading, zooming and switching views are free.

   | Drop the claw | Use a tool | Sort an item | Open a crate |
   |:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
   | 6 | 4 | 1 | free |
3. **What's free vs what's paid.** Free for every item: its name, what it looks
   like (`LOOKS LIKE:`), any printed marking in plain words such as "paper" or
   "cotton" (`MARKING:`), its weight, and three surface details. Paid:
   - `Rinse` washes a stain off. Food, drink and mud come away; chemicals
     don't.
   - `Chem Swab` names the stain, for example ketchup or pesticide.
   - `Magnifier` reads what the item is really made of.
   - `Cutter` opens `Sealed Crate`s, for free.

   A tool that finds nothing still costs 4.
4. **Four bins:** `ORGANIC` (things that rot, including cotton) · `RECYCLABLE`
   (clean paper, glass, metal and plastic, including polyester) · `HAZARDOUS`
   (anything soaked with a chemical that won't wash out) · `RESIDUE` (still
   stained, laminated, or used tissue). The bins are always clickable, and a
   bin **never says** whether you were right.
5. **Daily rules with priority:** "Still stained → RESIDUE" (10) is overridden
   by "Laminated → RESIDUE" (30), which is overridden by "Chemical stain →
   HAZARDOUS" (40). New rules arrive as the days go on, so the answer for the
   same item can change.

- **Say:** *"The claw decides **what** you have to judge. The desk decides **how
  much** it costs to judge it. Both draw on the same budget, so every drop is
  inspection you won't be able to afford later."*

### Slide 5: Game loop
Draw it as a cycle diagram:

```
 Day briefing ──▶ Claw machine ◀──Tab──▶ Sorting desk ──▶ End of day ──▶ next day
 (rules, quota,   (drop: 6)              (tools: 4,        (The Day: every mistake
  shift time,                             sort: 1)          explained; Technology:
  tools)                                                    build machine parts)
       ▲─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                     after Day 5 ──▶ one of 4 endings
```

- **Key point:** the claw and the desk are **two views of one shift**, not two
  phases. Switching is free.
- **The quota:** `End Shift` stays locked ("Sort {n} more") until the day's
  quota is met, and the quota counts **every** sort, right or wrong. That's
  deliberate satire (proposal §2.1.3): meeting a number isn't the same as doing
  the job well.

### Slide 6: Upgrades and progression
**a) Materials → machines.** Only correctly sorted items become material, and
material builds components, which build machines:

| Machine | Parts (materials) | Switches on |
|---|---|---|
| **Ocean Cleaning Drone** | Drone Shell (1 ORG + 2 REC), Rotor Skid (2 ORG + 1 RES) | **Fast Rail**: claw moves 60% faster |
| **Recycling Station** | Intake Arm (2 REC), Sorting Belt (3 RES), Press Housing (2 ORG + 2 REC) | **Strong Grip**: +15 grip chance |
| **Reef Restoration Pod** | Pod Shell (2 ORG + 3 RES), Seeding Arm (5 HAZ) | **Twin Arms**: tools cost 3, not 4 |

All three need 25 correct sorts (7 ORG, 6 REC, 5 HAZ, 7 RES), and the daily
quotas total 41. **At quota pace, more than 6 in 10 sorts must be right**, so a
player who guesses can't reach the best ending.

**b) Upgrades are earned, not bought.** There's no currency. A finished machine
switches itself on for the rest of the run, and the upgrades stack. Careful
sorting early buys more room late, when the quota is highest.

> ⚠️ Don't promise that Twin Arms changes how the game plays. It arrives too
> late to matter much (see §5, B4). Present the Reef Pod's reward as **the best
> ending**.

**c) The difficulty curve.** Show it as a small chart:

| Day | Quota | Shift time | Items | Crates | New this day |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 | 90 | 8 | 0 | Baseline rules only, no tools |
| 2 | 6 | 110 | 14 | 1 | Stain rule; Rinse, Magnifier, Cutter |
| 3 | 8 | 130 | 19 | 1 | Chemical rule; Chem Swab; hazardous items appear |
| 4 | 10 | 164 | 23 | 2 | Lamination rule |
| 5 | 12 | 174 | 26 | 2 | No new rule; more items |

The quota rises 140% from Day 1 to Day 5, while shift time rises only 93%.

**The answer changes between days:** the Used Toothbrush is `RECYCLABLE` on
Day 3 and `RESIDUE` on Day 4, when the lamination rule starts. The toothbrush
didn't change; the rules did. Anyone who memorised Day 3 gets it wrong.

**Endings:** there are four, one per number of machines built (0–3), each with
its own painting. Show the four `ending_N.png` thumbnails in a row, from
*"The Bay Was Left As It Was"* to *"The Bay Came Back"*.

### Slide 8: Theme reference
**Competition fit** (the proposal's framing):
- *Bloom Beyond*: one simple habit, **checking before deciding**, takes root in
  the player and goes with them when they leave the screen. The bay recovering
  in the endings is the "bloom".
- *Reach further*: all items, rules and days are data files separate from the
  code, so the lessons can be adapted to a specific region's sorting rules
  without rewriting the program.
- *Environment: responsible resource management*: material keeps its value only
  when sorted correctly. That rule is the win condition, not a backdrop.

**Visual and tonal references:**
- Setting: the player sits in the cockpit of a work submarine, and the sea
  exists only inside the window frame. This also explains why the player can't
  touch the trash directly and must use tools.
- Report and briefing screens look like blueprint sheets taped to a work desk.
- Art is hand-drawn in Procreate by the team, with the rounded Fredoka typeface
  and a calm, hopeful tone rather than a preachy one.
- Game references: *Papers, Please* for rule-based inspection under rules that
  change daily, and arcade claw machines for the grab. **Own the comparison
  before the judges raise it:** *"Papers, Please hands you the documents. We
  make you pay to see them."*

### B. Design loopholes that could undercut our main claim

**B1. Some item names give the answer away.** The name is free information.
*Chemical-Soaked Tissue* announces it's chemical, so the Chem Swab is
pointless for it. *Clean Paper Cup* tells you the other paper cup is the
suspicious one. The *Stained Shirt* ("stained woven fabric", "sharp smell") is
intentional (proposal §2.2.5: reading carefully already helps), but the tissue
and the cup aren't mentioned anywhere.
**At the final:** don't use the tissue or the clean cup in the demo. **v1.1:**
neutral names such as "Stained Tissue" and "Takeaway Cup".

**B2. Few items actually need a tool.** Running every item through each day's
rules, a player who only reads the free text can sort most items correctly:

| Needs a tool to be sure | Readable from free text |
|---|---|
| Frayed vs Faded Shirt (Magnifier), Paper Cup (the stain and liner are hidden), Used Toothbrush from Day 4 (Magnifier) | Fruit, leaves, eggshells, pizza, newspapers, bottle, clean cup, anything visibly stained (→ RESIDUE), both chemical items (smell and name) |

**At the final:** say "the deciding detail is *often* hidden", not "always",
and build every example around the items that really need tools: the shirts
and the Paper Cup.

**B3. "When in doubt, RESIDUE" is always correct for stained items, and Rinse
is never needed to be correct.** An unrinsed stained item goes to RESIDUE under
the stain rule, so dumping every visible stain in RESIDUE is always right,
except for the two chemical items, whose smell gives them away. Rinse only
turns a RESIDUE item into ORGANIC or RECYCLABLE material. The clean items in
the Day 1 and Day 2 pools alone hold 10 ORGANIC and 7 RECYCLABLE, more than all
three machines need (7 and 6). So **Rinse may never be worth 4**, even though
the proposal calls it the tool that best explains the message.
**At the final:** if a judge raises it, say *"That's true in real life too:
'when in doubt, leave it out' is standard recycling advice. What you lose is
the material you could have recovered."* The playtest (§6) will show whether
anyone rinses. **v1.1:** a cheaper Rinse, or higher ORG/REC needs.

**B4. The third machine's upgrade arrives too late.** The Reef Pod needs 5
HAZARDOUS. There are 2 hazardous items on Day 3 and 5 on Day 4, so the earliest
possible build is the end of Day 4. Twin Arms then helps on Day 5 at most, and
never if the Pod is built after Day 5.
**At the final:** don't sell Twin Arms as a reward (slide 6); the reward is the
ending. **v1.1:** move Twin Arms to an earlier machine.

**B5. Waiting is free, and the day's items are finite.** Items arrive every
8–14 real seconds from a fixed pool, and waiting costs no shift time. A patient
player can wait a few minutes, see the whole day's pool, then choose.
**If asked:** *"Thinking is free by design. We price decisions, not
reflexes."*

**B6. After you find the evidence, the game applies the rules for you.** Once a
tool reveals the key fact, the rule lights up and a ring points at the right
bin. Players learn *which fact to check*; they don't have to apply the rule
themselves.
**If asked:** *"The decision we train is what to check and whether it's worth
it. Lighting the rule next to the evidence shows the connection instead of
hiding it."*

**B7. Players can meet the quota by guessing** (the quota counts wrong sorts).
The proposal defends this as satire (§2.1.3). **Have that line ready**, because
a judge may see it as a bug.

**B8. An unopened Sealed Crate silently disappears** at the end of a shift.
**At the final:** open every crate in the demo run. **v1.1:** report it on the
end-of-day screen.

### C. Weak spots in the argument
These are all fixable, because they live in the deck, not in the game.

- **C1.** There's only one real statistic, and it's from 2015 (based on 2010
  data). Add the government's 2023 figure and the Surabaya case (§2).
- **C2.** The contamination claim behind the zero-value rule had no source. It's
  now backed by Surabaya 2019 and China 2018.
- **C3.** "Most people already care" has no source, and BPS 2018 suggests
  otherwise. Drop it and use the reframing in §2.
- **C4.** The proposal admits the playtest was small. A playtest of the
  **submitted** build is still possible, and it's new evidence about exactly
  the game being judged.

## 7. Demo plan

**Constraints:**
- Use **only the submitted build**: the itch.io download or the backed-up zip.
- The guided tour **can't be skipped**. It's 30 steps, and some steps wait for
  the player to act. It runs on the first run of every launch.
- `End Shift` stays locked until the day's quota is met, and there's no save.

So you can't open the game fresh on stage and reach the end-of-day screen in 3
minutes.

**Setup:**
1. **Main laptop:** before walking on stage, play a run into **Day 4** and stop
   at **8 of 10 sorted**, with at least 20 shift time left (the script spends
   17). Have the Muddy Shirt, the Stained Shirt and a Paper Cup unsorted in the
   inventory, and **every crate opened** (B8). Pause with `Esc` and **don't close
   the game**.
2. **Second laptop:** the same setup, prepared separately, so one crash doesn't
   end the demo.
3. **Backup:** the demo video you submitted (https://youtu.be/B8MtRwUGphM),
   downloaded to both laptops. It's already part of the submission, so it's the
   safest fallback. Don't use a debug build: it would be a new export, not the
   submitted game.

**Script (about 3 minutes):**
1. The cockpit: point out the calendar, the shift time and the quota.
2. Move the claw and drop it (−6).
3. Press `Tab` to go to the desk. Show the Muddy Shirt and the Stained Shirt
   side by side: *"Both stained cotton. Which one can we save?"*
4. Use the Chem Swab on the Stained Shirt (−4): pesticide. The chemical rule and
   the HAZARDOUS bin light up, so sort it there (−1).
5. Rinse the Muddy Shirt (−4): the mud washes off and it's ORGANIC again. Sort
   it (−1). That meets the quota.
6. Put the Paper Cup in RECYCLABLE **on purpose** and say: *"Looks like paper,
   marked paper."* Then press End Shift.
7. The end-of-day screen: the mistake card and its **`Never checked:`** line,
   then *"lost, not stored."*
8. The Technology tab: build a part, and show that the machine switches on an
   upgrade.
9. **Stop there.** Don't press *Start the Next Day*: it opens the Day 5
   briefing with the wrong "new foil rule" text (A4). Go back to the slides.

**Hardware checklist:** two laptops, a mouse, an HDMI/USB-C adapter, audio
output, and chargers. Test the projector resolution: the game is 1280×720.

## 8. Q&A prep

| Likely question | Answer outline |
|---|---|
| "Isn't this just Papers, Please with trash?" | In Papers, Please the information is handed to you for free. Here you **pay** for it and choose what to find out, and the claw competes for the same budget. |
| "Why is there no timer?" | A timer kills inspection, because nobody reads a label with a clock running. An action budget moves the pressure onto *is this worth finding out?* |
| "Why doesn't the bin tell me if I was right?" | Instant feedback teaches trial and error. Waiting until the end of the day, then showing `Never checked:`, teaches the checking process. |
| "Is the zero-value rule realistic?" | Yes. In Surabaya in 2019, shipments of "waste paper" were sent back whole because of what was mixed in, and China accepts at most 0.5% contamination. |
| "How do you know players learn anything?" | Show the playtest quiz results (§6). Without them: "That's what we're measuring next." |
| "I sorted everything dirty into RESIDUE and was always right." | B3: that's real advice too; what you lose is recoverable material. |
| "The tissue's name says it's chemical." | B1: "We caught that in our own review; it's the first item on our v1.1 list." |
| "Why didn't you fix these?" | "The submission is frozen for every team, which is fair. Here is exactly what v1.1 changes." Point to slide 12. |
| "Your proposal says to play in the browser, but there's no browser version." | A1: own it in one sentence. |
| "Day 5 announced a new rule that never came." | A4: the text is from an earlier design; it's on the v1.1 list. |
| "Why can I meet the quota by guessing?" | B7: satire; a system that counts numbers can't tell good work from bad. |
| "Only 5 days: what's the replay value?" | It's a complete one-sitting run with 4 endings, each run shuffles the item order, and more days are cheap to add. |
| "Why English if the problem is Indonesian?" | Localisation is on the roadmap, and all text lives in one file. |
| "Where are the resin codes you cite?" | A5: removed to keep it playable for everyone. |
| "Did you use AI?" | Answer accurately and in line with the guidebook's rules and what the proposal declared (§3.3 covers images and audio). Agree the wording as a team beforehand. |
